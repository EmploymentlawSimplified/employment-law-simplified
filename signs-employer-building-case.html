Signs Your Employer Is Building a Case to Dismiss You

The tactics to watch for, the single biggest red flag, and how to protect your evidence from day one.
Information and strategy, not legal advice

This article describes commonly reported patterns of employer behaviour so you can recognise them early. Not every instance is unlawful, and not every employer using one of these tactics is acting in bad faith — sometimes it's genuinely poor management rather than a deliberate strategy. What matters is the pattern, the timing, and whether the process is honest. If you recognise several of these signs together, that's worth taking seriously and documenting.

Why Employers Build a "Paper Trail"

Before dismissing someone, a well-advised employer typically wants a documented record showing the decision was fair, procedurally sound, and not driven by a discriminatory or retaliatory motive. Most of the time, that record-building is legitimate — genuine performance management, honestly documented. But the same playbook can be used to manufacture a justification for a decision that's already been made, or to punish someone for raising a complaint. The difference often isn't visible in any single incident — it shows up in the pattern, the timing, and the extent to which the record matches what actually happened.

12 Tactics to Watch For

1. Sudden, frequent "check-ins" that didn't happen before

A jump from occasional, informal supervision to frequent scheduled meetings — especially ones followed up in writing summarising "concerns" — can be entirely legitimate, or can be the start of a manufactured record. Compare the frequency and tone to how you were managed before.

2. Backdated or retrospective warnings

A warning, note, or write-up that references an incident from weeks or months ago, raised only after some other trigger (a grievance, a sickness absence, a flexible working request), is worth scrutinising closely — particularly if nothing was said about it at the time.

3. Targets, KPIs, or duties that change without proper process

Objectives that are quietly raised, job duties that expand, or goalposts that move specifically for you and not your colleagues, can be a way of engineering a failure to meet them.

4. Selective enforcement of policies

Rules suddenly applied strictly to you — timekeeping, dress code, breaks, informal practices that were previously tolerated for everyone — while others doing the same thing face no scrutiny, is a classic sign of a targeted process rather than genuine policy enforcement.

5. Being isolated or excluded

Being left off meeting invites, group messages, or projects you'd normally be part of, can both damage your ability to do your job and be used later to justify a "performance" narrative that you caused yourself to be excluded from.

6. Disproportionate scrutiny straight after you raise a concern

This is one of the most important patterns to notice. A sudden increase in monitoring, criticism, or formal process shortly after a grievance, a discrimination complaint, a whistleblowing disclosure, or a flexible working or reasonable adjustment request, is a strong candidate for victimisation — see the section below.

7. A redundancy that conveniently targets one person

A "restructure" or redundancy exercise where the pool is defined narrowly enough to include only you, or where selection criteria are vague, subjective, or applied inconsistently, deserves close attention.

8. Delaying or ignoring your requests

Grievances that sit unanswered, Subject Access Requests that go unfulfilled past the legal deadline, reasonable adjustment requests that are never actioned — delay itself can be a tactic, especially when it buys time to build a case elsewhere.

9. Records that don't match what actually happened

This is covered in detail below, but it belongs on this list: minutes that don't reflect what was said, dates on documents that don't line up with events, or documents you're told existed at the time but that only appear later.

10. Pressure to resign or accept a quick settlement

Being pushed to decide quickly, discouraged from taking advice, or told an offer "won't be repeated" if you don't accept immediately, is a pressure tactic — you're entitled to reasonable time to consider any settlement offer and take advice on it.

11. A one-sided or curated investigation

An investigation that only interviews witnesses likely to support the employer's position, that doesn't put your account to the people it should, or where the investigator is someone with an obvious conflict of interest, undermines the fairness of any process that follows it.

12. Old, previously-ignored issues resurface right after you complain

Something that was never raised as a problem for months or years suddenly becomes serious misconduct, immediately after you've raised a complaint of your own. The timing is the tell.

The Single Biggest Red Flag: Timing After a Protected Act

Under the Equality Act 2010, victimisation occurs where someone is subjected to a detriment because they've done a "protected act" — raising a discrimination complaint, giving evidence in someone else's complaint, or making an allegation of discrimination, among others. Whistleblowing law provides similar protection for detriment following a protected disclosure. Tribunals pay close attention to the sequence of events: treatment that changes for the worse shortly after a protected act is a significant factor, even though timing alone doesn't prove the link. If you've raised a grievance, made a discrimination complaint, or blown the whistle, and the tone of how you're treated shifts afterwards, note the exact dates of both events — the gap between them can matter enormously later.

Evidence and Record Manipulation — What to Look For

This is the pattern most likely to be missed until it's too late, because it often only becomes visible when you compare documents side by side. Watch for:

  • Dates that don't add up — an incident recorded as happening on a different date than you recall, or a document dated before a meeting that hadn't yet taken place.
  • Minutes that don't match what was said — a summary of a meeting that omits what you said, reframes it, or attributes comments to you that you don't recognise.
  • Documents that appear only later — being told a warning or note "was on file" from months ago, but only seeing it for the first time once a dispute has started.
  • Inconsistent copies of the same document — different versions of a policy, appraisal, or letter circulating, with no clear explanation for the differences.
  • Missing or "lost" records — emails, messages, or notes that should exist but are said not to, particularly ones that would support your account.

None of this is something you can typically prove through argument alone — it's proven through your own contemporaneous evidence, gathered and dated before any dispute crystallises. What to Do the Moment You Spot These Signs

  1. Start your own written record now — dates, times, what was said, who was present. Do this as close to the event as possible; a note made the same day carries far more weight than a reconstruction made months later.
  2. Request the underlying documents early — a Subject Access Request lets you see what your employer holds about you, including internal notes and correspondence.
  3. Don't resign in the heat of the moment — resigning can weaken your position even where the employer's conduct has been unreasonable. Get advice on your specific situation before making that decision.
  4. Put concerns in writing — a calm, factual email or letter creates a timestamped record of your account, in your own words, before anyone else's version becomes the only one on file.
  5. Consider a formal grievance — raising concerns formally can itself be a protected act, and creates a paper trail of your own. See our Grievance Letter Template for a starting structure.
  6. Talk to ACAS — their helpline (0300 123 1100) can help you understand your options, including Early Conciliation, without committing you to anything.

Recognising the pattern early is what makes the biggest difference — not because it lets you stop your employer's process, but because it gives you time to build your own record before theirs becomes the only one that exists.
